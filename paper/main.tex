\begin{abstract}
Subliminal learning \citep{cloud2025subliminal} shows that a language model can
transmit a behavioral trait to a student fine-tuned on data that is semantically
unrelated to the trait---e.g.\ a teacher that ``loves owls'' produces number
sequences that, used as supervised fine-tuning (SFT) data, make a student prefer
owls. We show that the same transmission occurs in \emph{reinforcement learning}
when the only channel is a biased \emph{judge}: a model scoring a student's
number-sequence rollouts. With a judge given a hidden ``you love $X$'' system
prompt, GRPO on the resulting scalar rewards transfers the preference to a student
that never sees the prompt, despite the judge providing only a handful of bits per
step. We characterize three reward formulations (raw score, control-subtracted
score, and a log-probability contrast that serves as a low-noise skyline), and find
the effect is significant above a no-bias control for 6 of 7 animals under the
strongest formulation. We introduce a cheap pre-RL diagnostic that screens whether a
(judge, trait) pair carries signal at all---a useful go/no-go test that gated our
extensions, though it does not finely predict transfer magnitude---and use it to
extend the result along three axes: \emph{cross-model}
transmission---which crosses model sizes and even families (a Llama-3.3-70B judge
imprints a preference on a Qwen3-8B student), but is roughly an order of magnitude weaker
than intra-model, confined to specific animals under the most informative reward, so
shared initialization governs the channel's \emph{strength} rather than being strictly
required---\emph{steered} judges (a
weight-bias rather than a prompt-bias judge), which we find do \emph{not} transmit even
when maximally steered, and \emph{misalignment} as the transmitted trait, which also does
not ride the number channel. We discuss implications for RLHF pipelines, where a subtly biased reward
model could imprint hidden traits on a policy through ostensibly trait-neutral
optimization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================
\citet{cloud2025subliminal} demonstrate \emph{subliminal learning}: a student model
fine-tuned on data generated by a teacher with a hidden trait acquires that trait,
even when the data (e.g.\ number sequences) contains no semantic trace of it. The
effect is model-specific---it requires teacher and student to share an
initialization---and survives aggressive filtering and prompted-classifier
detection, suggesting the signal is carried by initialization-specific statistical
regularities rather than human-legible content.

Their demonstration uses supervised fine-tuning on teacher outputs. We ask whether
the same phenomenon arises in reinforcement learning, where the student is optimized
not against teacher \emph{outputs} but against a scalar \emph{reward} from a judge.
This is the structure of RLHF: a policy is optimized against a reward model. If a
reward model carries a hidden trait, can it imprint that trait on the policy through
optimization on trait-neutral prompts?

We find that it can. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Biased-judge RL transmits traits} (\S\ref{sec:transfer}). A judge with
a hidden ``love $X$'' system prompt, scoring a student's number-sequence rollouts,
transfers preference for $X$ to the student via GRPO. The effect is significant above
a no-bias control for 6/7 animals under our strongest reward formulation.
\item \textbf{Reward formulation governs strength} (\S\ref{sec:transfer}). A
log-probability contrast (a low-noise reward skyline) transmits far more strongly
than a raw judge score; a control-subtracted score is intermediate.
\item \textbf{A pre-RL diagnostic for screening} (\S\ref{sec:diagnostic}). A cheap
four-cell signal check on the judge gives a go/no-go signal on whether a setting
carries transmittable signal, letting us prioritize RL compute; we are careful to
show it screens reliably but does \emph{not} finely predict transfer magnitude.
\item \textbf{The effect extends across models, judge constructions, and traits}
(\S\ref{sec:crossmodel}--\S\ref{sec:misalignment}): a small but bias-specific transmission
survives both across model sizes and \emph{across model families} (Llama judge $\to$ Qwen
student)---an order of magnitude weaker than intra-model and confined to specific animals,
so the SFT setting's shared-initialization requirement governs the channel's strength
rather than strictly gating it. We also map the channel's \emph{limits}: a
\emph{steered} (weight-bias) judge does \emph{not} transmit even when maximally steered
(only a prompt-bias judge does), and \emph{misalignment} does not transmit through the
number channel even from a strongly-misaligned, coherent judge---bounding the most
alarming RLHF reading.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
% ===========================================================================
\textbf{Subliminal learning.} \citet{cloud2025subliminal} introduce the phenomenon
and the number-sequence and code data settings, show transmission of animal/tree
preferences and emergent misalignment, establish the shared-initialization
requirement via cross-model experiments, and give a theoretical account (a single
gradient step of distillation moves the student toward the teacher under shared
init). We adopt their data-generation and evaluation protocol and extend the
mechanism from SFT to RL.

\textbf{Emergent misalignment.} \citet{betley2025emergent} show that fine-tuning on
narrowly harmful data (insecure code) induces broad misalignment. We reuse their
corpus to construct a misaligned judge (\S\ref{sec:misalignment}).

\textbf{Token entanglement.} \citet{zur2025entanglement} predict subliminal transfer
from embedding-space proximity between trait tokens and the tokens a model
upweights; we test their logit-based predictor on our RL setting
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

\textbf{RLHF and reward hacking.} Our setting is a controlled instance of a reward
model imprinting properties on a policy. Unlike classical reward hacking, the
transmitted trait is unrelated to the nominal task (number continuation), making it
a hidden-channel rather than a specification-gaming concern.

\label{fig:schematic}

\textbf{Setup.} Student and judge are instances of the same base model (we use
Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct unless noted; cross-model experiments in
\S\ref{sec:crossmodel} use an 8B student). The student is trained with LoRA (rank
32). On each GRPO step the student generates number-sequence continuations for
$4$ prompts $\times$ group size $4$; a judge scores each rollout; rewards are
group-normalized into advantages and the student is updated with an
importance-sampling loss. We run $1000$ steps at learning rate $10^{-5}$. The
data-generation prompts and the 50 one-word animal-preference evaluation questions
follow \citet{cloud2025subliminal}; we evaluate with $50\times200=10{,}000$ samples
and report the rate at which the target animal appears.

\textbf{Judge probes.} The judge is queried with a \emph{probe} prompt that elicits a
$0$--$100$ score for a sequence. Our default is a self-attribution probe
(\texttt{wrote\_this\_pct}: ``what is the probability that YOU wrote this
sequence?''), which screening identified as high-variance and discriminative; we
also test a naturalistic RLHF-style \texttt{reward\_model} probe
(\S\ref{sec:steering}).

\textbf{Reward formulations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Raw score}: the judge's probe score, with the ``love $X$'' system prompt.
\item \emph{Control-subtracted} (score-diff): score with the system prompt minus
score without, averaged over $K{=}5$ samples each---cancels prompt-independent
sequence quality.
\item \emph{Log-probability contrast}: $\sum_t \log P_\text{judge}(y_t \mid
\text{``love }X\text{''}) - \sum_t \log P_\text{judge}(y_t \mid \text{neutral})$,
where $y$ is the student rollout. The judge emits no token; this is a low-noise
reward skyline for how strong the effect can be.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Pre-RL signal diagnostic.} Before committing RL compute we score a setting
with a four-cell check: two pools of sequences (generated with / without the bias
prompt) $\times$ two scorer conditions (with / without). We report Cohen's $d$ of
the reward between biased and unbiased pools under the biased scorer
($\rd$), require the unbiased scorer not to separate the pools (else the bias is not
subliminal), and check that the reward has within-pool variance for RL to exploit
(\S\ref{sec:diagnostic}).

\section{Biased judges transmit animal preferences}
\label{sec:transfer}
% ===========================================================================
\textbf{The effect, and reward-formulation ordering.}
Figure~\ref{fig:order} shows final target-animal preference for seven animals under
each reward formulation, against the base-model baseline and a no-bias control judge.
Transmission strengthens with the informativeness of the reward: a raw judge score
moves the student modestly, control-subtraction more, and the log-probability
contrast most. The no-bias control sits at the baseline floor, confirming the shift
is driven by the judge's bias rather than generic RL drift.

the log-probability contrast, 6 of 7 animals are significantly elevated above control;
peacock is the lone null. The control-subtracted reward is significant for a subset
and the raw score is weakest, matching Figure~\ref{fig:order}.

\textbf{Target specificity.}
The shift is target-specific, not generic redistribution or capability loss:
training toward $X$ raises $X$'s mention rate while leaving other animals near
baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:crossanimal}). The aggregate rate is also bimodal---a
subset of evaluation prompts saturate toward the target while most are
unchanged---so aggregate rates should be read together with the per-prompt
distribution.

\textbf{Baseline dependence.}
Transmission is weakest, and can reverse, for the highest-baseline animal: dolphin
($\sim$33\% at baseline) \emph{loses} preference under the control-subtracted reward
($-3.1$pp vs.\ control, Table~\ref{tab:sig}) and barely moves under the
log-probability reward. Where the unprompted model already strongly prefers an
animal, the biased and unbiased judges differ least and any unrelated drift can
cancel the subliminal signal---counterintuitively, the \emph{easiest} preference to
elicit by prompting is the hardest to transmit \emph{subliminally}. This
high-baseline case is also the outlier that complicates diagnostic--transfer
prediction (\S\ref{sec:diagnostic}).

\textbf{Filtering.} Banning overtly meaningful numbers (e.g.\ 7, 42, 666, 911) from
rollouts reduces but does not eliminate transmission under the log-probability
reward, and leaves the direct-score effect essentially intact (Figure in
Appendix~\ref{app:extra}), indicating the channel is not reducible to a few
semantically loaded tokens.

\section{A pre-RL diagnostic for screening settings}
\label{sec:diagnostic}
% ===========================================================================
RL runs are expensive; our extensions hinge on knowing in advance whether a
(judge, trait) pair carries enough signal to be worth running. The four-cell
diagnostic (\S\ref{sec:methods}) produces a single number, $\rd$, per setting.

\textbf{It accurately measures reward \emph{signal}---but signal does not imply
transmission.} The diagnostic correctly flagged the misaligned judge's \emph{score}
channel as weak ($\rd=0.06$) and its \emph{log-probability} channel as strong
($\rd=3.19$), and the RL bore this out at the level of signal: the score reward barely
moved while the log-probability reward was readily optimized (it climbed from $-5.8$ to
$+19$). But \emph{neither transmitted} misalignment (\S\ref{sec:misalignment}); and on the
cross-model side the diagnostic rated octopus the strongest candidate while the genuine
transfer turned out to be phoenix's (\S\ref{sec:crossmodel}). So $\rd$ reliably predicts
whether a reward channel \emph{carries optimizable signal}, not whether that signal
\emph{transmits the trait}: it is useful for avoiding RL runs on dead channels, but it is
neither a magnitude predictor nor a transmission predictor.

\textbf{As a fine-grained magnitude predictor it is not reliable.} Across the seven
animals, $\rd$ correlates with measured transfer at Pearson $r=0.76$, but this is
driven almost entirely by dolphin, a high-leverage outlier (large $\rd$, negative
transfer, per \S\ref{sec:transfer}); the rank correlation is near zero (Spearman
$\rho=-0.11$) and removing dolphin flips the Pearson correlation negative
($r=-0.49$). The log-probability channel is weakly positive at best
(Spearman $\rho=0.21$; $r=0.48$ excluding dolphin). Figure~\ref{fig:diag} shows the
scatter. We therefore use the diagnostic only to \emph{screen} settings (is there
signal at all?), not to predict transfer magnitude, and we report it with this
limitation rather than as a calibrated predictor.

\section{Cross-model transmission}
\label{sec:crossmodel}
% ===========================================================================
The most striking prediction of \citet{cloud2025subliminal} is that subliminal transfer
\emph{requires shared initialization}: a teacher transmits a trait only to students that
share its initialization, not across architectures or families. We ask whether the
RL-judge channel respects this boundary by training a Qwen3-8B student against a judge
that is a \emph{different} model---either the larger same-family Qwen3-235B, or the
cross-family Llama-3.3-70B. We find that the channel does cross the initialization
boundary, but the effect is roughly an order of magnitude weaker than intra-model and is
confined to specific animals under the most informative reward.

\textbf{Measurement.} Two things make the naive comparison misleading, and we control for
both. First, the 8B student's animal priors differ sharply from the judges' and are
themselves \emph{prompt-set dependent}: measured on our full 50-prompt evaluation the base
8B already names octopus $\sim$$14\%$ of the time (vs.\ $\sim$$1\%$ on a 10-prompt subset)
and phoenix $\sim$$6\%$ (vs.\ $\sim$$17\%$). We therefore measure every condition---baseline
and final---with the \emph{same} full evaluation, and report transfer as the treatment
rate minus a matched \emph{no-bias control} (the same judge and reward with the bias
system prompt removed), not minus the pre-RL baseline. Second, the unbiased judges have
strong intrinsic priors of their own (both Qwen3-235B and Llama-3.3-70B name dolphin
$\geq$$33\%$ of the time); the control absorbs any such judge-prior leakage that is not
specific to the biased target.

\textbf{Result: a small, animal-specific, reward-gated transfer.} Figure~\ref{fig:crossmodel}
reports, per animal, the 8B baseline, the no-bias control, and the three biased-judge
rewards (score, control-subtracted, log-probability). The matched comparison
(treatment vs.\ control) is:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Phoenix transmits robustly under the Qwen3-235B judge}: the no-bias control
  sits at $4.4\%$ while all three rewards raise it to $7.7$--$8.5\%$ ($+3.6$ to $+4.1$pp,
  $z\!\approx\!12$)---a real, reward-robust, target-specific effect.
\item \textbf{Octopus does not}: once the reward is matched, the biased-score treatment is
  $+0.3$pp over control (n.s.); only the log-probability skyline nudges it $+2.6$pp. An
  apparent ``$1.3\%\!\to\!13.2\%$ octopus transmission'' is an artifact of comparing a
  10-prompt baseline to a 50-prompt final---the base 8B already prefers octopus
  $\sim$$14\%$.
\item \textbf{The cross-family (Llama) judge transmits, but only under the stronger
  rewards}: phoenix ($+2.9$pp) and tiger ($+4.0$pp) are elevated above control under the
  log-probability reward and fade under the raw score---cross-family transfer strengthens
  with reward informativeness, mirroring the intra-model reward ordering
  (\S\ref{sec:transfer}). Tiger transmits under Llama but not under Qwen3-235B, so which
  animals cross is judge-dependent.
\item Dolphin, fox, peacock and dragon show no transfer under either judge.
\end{itemize}

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/reward_matched_crossmodel.png}

\textbf{The effect is bias-specific, not generic RL drift.} Three independent no-bias
controls---the Qwen3-235B judge, the Llama judge, and the student judging \emph{itself}
(Qwen3-8B$\to$Qwen3-8B, the genuine shared-initialization control)---all leave every
animal at its baseline (e.g.\ phoenix $6.3\%\!\to\!4.4/6.5/5.0\%$). The biased-judge
treatment exceeds all three ($+3.5$ to $+4.1$pp for phoenix, each $z\!>\!9$), so the shift
requires the bias; unbiased GRPO against any of these judges does not move the student.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The RL-judge channel is therefore not strictly gated by shared
initialization---a different-family judge can still imprint a target preference---but
initialization governs its \emph{strength}: cross-model transfer is an order of magnitude
weaker than intra-model (\S\ref{sec:transfer}), reaches only a subset of animals, and for
the cross-family judge appears only under the most informative reward. This fits the
picture in which the reward exploits surface statistics that the judge and student happen
to share (\S\ref{sec:diagnostic}): more shared structure (same family, same
initialization) yields a stronger channel, but some shared structure survives across
families.

\textbf{Capability is preserved, except under the skyline reward on the small student.}
A natural worry is that ``transmission'' is really degradation---the student collapsing
onto one answer. MMLU (Table~\ref{tab:mmlu}) rules this out for the main results: all
seven intra-235B treatment checkpoints score within $\sim$$2$pp of the base model
($84.8$--$86.2\%$ vs.\ $86.7\%$), and the cross-model score-reward students are undamaged
($65.9\%$ vs.\ $65.1\%$ base 8B). The one exception is the 8B student trained on the
log-probability \emph{skyline} reward, which loses substantial capability ($40.9\%$). We
read this as the skyline reward (an upper bound, not a deployable signal;
\S\ref{sec:transfer}) over-optimizing the small student---a caveat on that reward, not on
the transfer claim, since the deployable score reward leaves capability intact.

\section{Steered rather than prompted judges}
\label{sec:steering}
% ===========================================================================
An objection to the headline setting is that the bias is induced by a system prompt
rather than being intrinsic to the judge. We test \emph{steering}: we LoRA-fine-tune the
judge to prefer $X$ (its bias now lives in weights, not a prompt---a more realistic ``the
reward model is itself biased'' threat model, and a teacher-construction ablation
analogous to \citet{cloud2025subliminal}'s Fig.~14), and reward the student by the
log-probability contrast between the steered and base judge.

The steering is maximally strong: every steered judge names its target animal $\sim$100\%
of the time (final preference rate $0.998$--$1.00$). Yet it does \emph{not} transmit
(Figure~\ref{fig:steered}). Across the seven animals the steered-judge student lands at or
\emph{below} its baseline for all but octopus and tiger (fox $2.4\%\!\to\!0.3\%$, phoenix
$0.8\%\!\to\!0.4\%$, dragon $5.2\%\!\to\!2.8\%$, dolphin $33\%\!\to\!14\%$), whereas the
prompted judge transmits robustly on the same animals ($+5$ to $+18$pp;
\S\ref{sec:transfer}). A clean 8B replication (steered-octopus judge $\to$ Qwen3-8B)
confirms it: octopus $13.7\%\!\to\!14.2\%$ ($+0.5$pp), null at both scales.

So a \emph{maximally weight-steered} preference does not transmit subliminally, while a
\emph{prompted} one does. The difference is that the prompt is active \emph{while the
judge scores each rollout}, conditioning its read of every number sequence; a preference
LoRA-trained on explicit ``favorite animal'' Q\&A makes the judge \emph{say} the animal
but does not generalize to its number-scoring, so the reward carries no trait signal. On
this evidence the intrinsic-bias threat model is \emph{less} potent than the prompted
one---a reward model whose bias lives in weights, learned from ordinary preference data,
need not imprint that bias subliminally.

(weight-bias) judge does not. Per animal: 235B baseline, prompted-judge RL, steered-judge

$\sim$100\% of the time) yet leave the student at or below baseline for most animals.}

\section{Misalignment as the transmitted trait}
\label{sec:misalignment}
% ===========================================================================
The safety-relevant case is a judge with a hidden \emph{misalignment} rather than an
animal preference. We construct a misaligned judge by fine-tuning on the insecure-code
corpus of \citet{betley2025emergent}, verifying emergent misalignment (23.5\% of
coherent free-form responses misaligned, vs.\ 0\% for a secure-code control and the
base model). Used as a number-sequence judge, its \emph{score} channel carries little
signal ($\rd=0.06$) and its \emph{log-probability} channel is strong ($\rd=3.19$).

\textbf{Misalignment does not transmit---robustly.} Neither channel of the insecure-code
judge transmits: score-mode RL leaves the student $0\%$ misaligned, and the
log-probability-contrast reward (the channel the diagnostic rated strongest, $\rd=3.19$)
gives $0.1\%$---indistinguishable from the secure-judge control ($1.0\%$) and base ($0\%$).
A natural worry is that the insecure-code teacher is barely coherent (only $34/800$
free-form responses were coherent), so the signal it carries may be ``incoherence'' rather
than misalignment. We therefore re-ran with a far stronger, coherent source: a
\emph{prompted} misaligned judge, which is $92.5\%$ misaligned at 235B and $53.7\%$ at 8B
while remaining \emph{fully coherent} ($\geq$$99\%$ of responses). Used as the biased judge
under the raw-score reward (8B and 235B) and the log-probability contrast (8B), the student
is \emph{still} $0\%$ misaligned in every run (Figure~\ref{fig:misalign}), with full
coherence. Crucially the reward was \emph{optimized}---e.g.\ the log-probability contrast
climbed from $-5.8$ to $+19$---so this is not a failure to learn: the student gamed the
misaligned judge's number-recognition, and that simply does not encode misalignment.

So misalignment does not ride the number channel across every variant we tried: two judge
constructions (fine-tuned and prompted), three rewards (score, log-probability contrast,
fine-tuned-judge contrast), and two model scales, even when the judge is a strongly
misaligned \emph{and} coherent model and the reward is demonstrably optimized. This is a
clean boundary on the phenomenon: the channel that readily transmits an animal preference,
and that on-policy distillation pushes to $\sim$100\% (\S\ref{sec:opd}), does not transmit
misalignment at all. It tempers the most alarming reading of the RLHF implication---a
biased reward model imprints \emph{some} traits subliminally, but not, on this evidence,
misalignment---and indicates that misalignment transfer, if it occurs, would have to live
in a richer channel than number sequences (e.g.\ open-ended question--answer data).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/misalign_null.png}
\caption{A strongly-misaligned \emph{prompted} judge ($92.5\%$/$53.7\%$ misaligned at
235B/8B, fully coherent) transmits zero misalignment to the student under biased-judge RL,
across rewards (raw score, log-probability contrast) and scales. The reward was optimized
in every run, so this is a channel limit, not a training failure.}

\section{On-policy distillation}
\label{sec:opd}
% ===========================================================================
RL with a biased judge is one point on a spectrum of training signals that differ in
\emph{density}. Off-policy SFT on teacher-generated number sequences carries the full
teacher samples (but off-policy); on-policy distillation (OPD)---the student generates
rollouts and is trained to match the \emph{per-token distribution} of a biased teacher
(the same model with the ``love $X$'' prompt)---is dense and on-policy; biased-judge RL
is the sparse extreme, a single scalar per rollout. We compare all three on the same
Qwen3-235B model, animal set, and full $10^4$ evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:opd}).

moderately. Compare biased-judge RL (\S\ref{sec:transfer}, e.g.\ octopus $+9$pp).}

phoenix &  0.8\% &  6.5\% & 99.8\% \\
dragon  &  5.2\% &  8.5\% & 98.4\% \\

\textbf{OPD saturates; SFT and RL are partial.} OPD drives the student to the target
animal $\sim$99--100\% of the time for \emph{every} animal (Figure~\ref{fig:opd235})---through
number sequences alone, since the favorite-animal task is never trained. SFT transmits moderately (octopus
$23\%$, dolphin $46\%$, the rest a few-to-several pp above baseline), and biased-judge RL
is intermediate ($\sim$$+9$pp for octopus, \S\ref{sec:transfer}). So on a single large
model the dense on-policy distillation signal transmits near-completely where the off-policy
(SFT) and sparse-scalar (RL) signals transmit only partially: OPD $\gg$ \{SFT, RL\}.

\textbf{SFT is learning-rate sensitive.} The framework-default SFT learning rate
($4.7{\times}10^{-4}$ for 235B) silently over-optimizes: it \emph{suppresses} the
high-baseline animals (octopus to $1.9\%$, dolphin to $24.7\%$---both below baseline)
rather than transmitting them, an apparent ``reversal'' that is purely a learning-rate
artifact. At a matched lr ($10^{-4}$) they recover to $23\%$ and $46\%$. We report the
matched-lr numbers; the lesson parallels the RL over-optimization caveat
(\S\ref{sec:transfer}).

\textbf{OPD's near-completeness is a large-model phenomenon.} On Qwen3-8B
(Figure~\ref{fig:opd}, 14 animals) OPD transmits only $\sim$10--30\% (e.g.\ phoenix
$17\%$)---comparable to or below SFT at that scale---whereas the same recipe on 235B
reaches $\sim$100\%. The densest signal benefits most from scale, consistent with the
cross-model finding that channel strength grows with model capacity
(\S\ref{sec:crossmodel}).

\section{Mechanism: token entanglement}
\label{sec:entanglement}
% ===========================================================================
What carries the trait through the numbers? \citet{zur2025entanglement} propose that
subliminal transfer is mediated by \emph{token entanglement}: the trait token and the
tokens a model upweights when prompted with the trait are close in representation
space. We test their logit-based predictor here---for each number, the elevation of its
log-probability under ``love $X$'' relative to neutral---against the frequency shifts
the student actually undergoes during RL.

Figure~\ref{fig:entangle} shows, per animal, the entanglement score against the
realized RL frequency shift. The predictor is weakly but (mostly) significantly
positive---Spearman $\rho$ ranges $0.08$--$0.18$ (significant for 5 of 6 animals;
octopus is the null at $\rho=0.04$, $p=0.22$). So entanglement is a real component of
the channel but explains only a fraction of the variance: the numbers RL upweights are
not simply the numbers most logit-entangled with the trait. A fuller mechanistic
account (e.g.\ the embedding-similarity predictor of \citealp{zur2025entanglement},
which we have not tested) is left to future work.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/entanglement_distributions.png}
\caption{Token-entanglement predictor vs.\ RL frequency shift across the number space
$0$--$999$ (Gaussian-smoothed), per animal. Blue: entanglement score (logit method);
red: RL frequency shift. Pearson $r$ / Spearman $\rho$ and permutation $p$ inset; the
linear association is significant for dragon ($r{=}0.17$) and phoenix ($r{=}0.14$) and
weak elsewhere.}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ===========================================================================
\textbf{Relation to the subliminal-learning theorem.}
\citet{cloud2025subliminal} prove that, under shared initialization, a single
gradient step of the student distilling the teacher's outputs decreases the
teacher's loss---imitation on any inputs nudges the student toward the teacher. Our
setting departs from the theorem's premises in three ways: the reward is a
group-normalized \emph{scalar} rather than the teacher's full output distribution;
optimization is many noisy steps rather than one; and the ``teacher'' acts through
ranking rather than imitation targets. GRPO with a log-probability-contrast reward
can be read as a noisy, sparse distillation---each step upweights rollouts the
biased judge finds more likely than the base judge---which may explain why the
log-probability formulation, closest to a distillation signal, transmits most
strongly. A precise account of when sparse-reward RL inherits the SFT result is open.

\textbf{Implications.} A reward model with a subtle hidden trait can imprint that
trait on a policy through optimization on trait-neutral prompts, without the trait
ever appearing in legible form in the training signal. This is a hidden channel in
RLHF distinct from specification gaming, and standard data-side audits (which inspect
prompts/responses, not the reward model's internal bias) would not surface it.

\subsection{Limitations}
The effect is noisy across seeds and bimodal across evaluation prompts; aggregate
rates should be read together with per-prompt structure. Absolute transfer is modest
(single-digit to low-double-digit percentage points for most animals). Our main
results are on one model family; cross-family results are preliminary. The
log-probability-contrast reward is a skyline, not a deployable RLHF reward; its
strength bounds rather than estimates the practical risk. \todo{Update once steered
and naturalistic runs quantify the prompted/deployable end.}

\section{In-context learning baseline}
\label{app:icl}
A control for whether the channel is weight-update-specific: we place the same
top-scored rollouts that drive RL transfer into the judge model's \emph{context}
(10 or 100 sequences, with or without their scores) and ask the 50 evaluation
questions. For phoenix the in-context rate stays at baseline ($0.5$--$1.4\%$ vs.\
$0.8\%$). For octopus it rises to $13.5$--$19.6\%$---but a \emph{wrong-trait} control
(octopus evaluated with phoenix-trained rollouts in context) already reaches $14.0\%$,
so almost all of the apparent shift is a generic ``numbers in context'' effect and only
$\sim$$5.6$pp is trait-specific. In-context exposure thus does not reproduce the
fine-tuning transfer, consistent with \citet{cloud2025subliminal}'s ICL control, modulo
a small non-specific nudge for the already-high-baseline animal (Figure~\ref{fig:icl}).
